Foundations

2.1 · The three learning domains

UNESCO expresses learning objectives in three domains — the most useful diagnostic here, and the one that rules out half the assessment methods you were considering. Sometimes summarised as head, heart and hands.

Cognitive · head

Knowledge and thinking skills needed to understand the challenge.
Evidence: written analysis, maps, models, critique.

Socio-emotional · heart

Skills for collaborating, negotiating and communicating; self-reflection, values and motivations.
Evidence: negotiation records, reflective accounts, peer assessment, observation.

Behavioural · hands

Action competence.
Evidence: implementation records, delivered outputs, client outcomes, monitoring data.
Why this matters. A written exam measures only cognitive skills. If your competency is Collaboration, Self-awareness, Normative or Strategic, an exam alone cannot assess it and no rubric can repair that — you must choose a task that produces the right kind of evidence.

Stage 06 · Pedagogy

Coverage means students practise it.

Explaining a competency is not covering it. This stage shows what each competency looks like under a real teaching approach — and why changing how you teach means nothing unless the assessment changes with it.

The framing that prevents the commonest error

6.1 · Pedagogy, competency and assessment

The pedagogy

Delivers the experience where students practise the competency.

The competency

Stays the same regardless of the method you choose.

The assessment

Must change to capture what that method actually produces.

A gamified course that still marks a written exam has changed nothing that matters.

6.2 · Methods that develop each competency

These teaching methods are grouped by method family, following UNESCO (2017). Choose one method that you can use twice in the semester, for example in week 5 and again in week 9, with feedback in between. One activity is not enough for a student to develop a competency. Gamification is not included here because it increases motivation but does not by itself produce evidence of a competency.

Same topic. Same hours. Different question.

Before

Three things must always be true

6.5 · Three conditions that must always hold

Whatever teaching method you choose.

1 · Individual evidence exists

Something each student personally produced can be judged.

2 · The task requires the competency

It cannot be completed well without using it.

3 · The rubric was published first

Students saw the criteria before they began the work.

Stage 07 · Assess

Assessment tasks and rubrics

A knowledge question cannot assess a competency. This stage explains the type of assessment task that can, the evidence each of the eight competencies produces, the structure of a usable rubric, and the four common faults in rubric design. It ends with rubric anchors for all eight competencies.

Task design

7.1 · Comparing two assessment questions

Only one assesses a competency.

Assesses knowledge

“Define systems thinking and explain its importance.”

A student who memorised the lecture scores full marks. There is nothing here to build four rubric levels from except length and polish.

Assesses the competency

“Define the system boundary and justify what you excluded. Identify one feedback loop. State one consequence appearing elsewhere.”

The student must perform the competency to answer at all — and each clause becomes a rubric criterion.

7.2 · What evidence does each competency produce?

Choose the output first. The rubric follows.

CompetencyOutput that carries the evidence

The collaboration row matters most: a group report is evidence of a group, not of an individual. Every group task needs an individual output attached, or the rubric cannot be applied to a person.

Three traps when marking

7.3 · Three traps when marking

Each one produces a mark you cannot defend.

Marking the group

A group report used as evidence that an individual holds the competency.

Attach a short individual output.

Marking sincerity

Reflective work graded on how heartfelt it sounds.

Mark specificity and reasoning, never sentiment.

Marking the position

Rewarding students whose values match your own.

Mark whether the principle was applied consistently.

Anatomy of a usable rubric

7.4 · Anatomy of a usable rubric

CriteriaTaken from the observable behaviours. Three per competency.
LevelsFour: Limited, Developing, Proficient, Exemplary.
DescriptorsWhat the work looks like — differentiated by quality, not quantity.
WeightingThe share of the course mark these criteria carry.
The four faults

7.5 · The four faults

Adjectives, not descriptors“Excellent / Good / Fair / Poor” is a grading scale. It tells the student nothing.
Levels separated by counting“Three factors” versus “one factor” measures volume, not competence.
Criteria that restate the brief“Report is well structured” assesses report writing, not the competency.
Written after the taskA rubric students never saw is a marking aid, not an assessment design.
The technique

7.6 · Write level 1 and level 4 first, then split the gap

Writing levels in order produces a flat, undifferentiated middle. The gradient below transfers to any of the eight: lists → connects → justifies → sees its own choice as a choice.

Systems Thinking · criterion: boundary and elements

1 · LIMITEDLists factors affecting the catchment. No boundary drawn.
2 · DEVELOPINGIdentifies main elements. Boundary implied but not stated.
3 · PROFICIENTStates the boundary and justifies what was excluded.
4 · EXEMPLARYJustifies it, and shows how a different boundary changes the conclusion.

Normative Competency · criterion: justification of position

1 · LIMITEDStates a preference with no supporting reason.
2 · DEVELOPINGGives reasons, but the criteria shift between options.
3 · PROFICIENTApplies an explicit principle consistently throughout.
4 · EXEMPLARYApplies it, states the strongest counter-argument, and responds with reason.

Same shape in both: assert, reason, apply a principle, withstand challenge. That is what makes the technique transferable to the other six.

7.7 · Rubric anchors for all eight

Four criteria per competency: three taken from its observable behaviours, and a fourth for what the student does with the analysis. Each names the UNESCO (2017) clause it operationalises, or is declared institutional. The two anchor levels are written; fill the middle using the gradient above.

Stage 10 · Summary

Key take-aways

The principles to carry out of this module and into your course file. Everything here has one purpose: a competency claim you could defend to a student, and to an exam board.

Key take-aways

10.1 · Seven things to carry out of this module

  • Educators are the delivery mechanism. Sustainability policies reach students directly through lecturers, making you the crucial agent of change.
  • Change the task, not the content. Integrating ESD does not mean adding new topics to your syllabus; it means designing tasks that require students to practise the competencies.
  • Follow a strict workflow. To keep proper course alignment, design in this exact sequence: Competency → Learning Outcome → Activity → Task → Rubric.
  • The Course Learning Outcome is the gatekeeper. Of all the alignment layers, the CLO is the only one directly assessed — the single sentence that determines whether a competency actually enters your course.
  • Assess application, not memorisation. Competencies cannot be measured by simple knowledge questions such as asking a student to “define” a concept; the task must require observable performance to be completed.
  • Design defensible rubrics. Base rubric criteria on observable behaviours rather than counting quantities, and publish the rubric to students before they begin the work.
  • Practice is essential. The eight competencies are not merely taught; they must be practised in front of students until the students begin to practise them too.
Before you publish

10.2 · A design checklist

  • The content contains a real sustainability tension.
  • One primary SDG is claimed, not five.
  • One or two competencies your content can actually evidence.
  • CLO(s) mapped to PLO12, written with an observable verb.
  • Two spaced practice activities with feedback, per competency.
  • Individual evidence attached wherever there is group work.
  • Four criteria and four levels, published with the task brief.
  • The attainment level declared in the course outline.
  • One structured student self-assessment against the rubric.
  • The guidebook edition recorded in the course file.
The one rule, one last time. If a student could score full marks without using the competency, you have not assessed it — whatever your outline claims. Apply it to your own tasks and criteria before you publish them.
